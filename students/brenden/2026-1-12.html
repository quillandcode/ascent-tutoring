The Discovery Session

Every first session is part assessment, part treasure hunt. You're looking for what a student knows, but more importantly—how they think. With Brenden, I found something encouraging right away: a student who doesn't shy from challenge.

We dove straight into calculus concepts he hasn't formally studied yet. I drew an S-curve representing water filling a container, and without hesitation, Brenden explained how the filling rate changed along the curve. Another curve showed a car's motion—he immediately grasped that velocity was key, identifying acceleration at different points. This wasn't memorized material. This was intuitive understanding of rate of change.

In our relaxed environment, Brenden performed at a solid mid-to-high B level—nowhere near C territory. The gap between that performance and his current grade tells me this isn't a knowledge problem. It's a pressure problem.

Core Strengths

Brenden has several natural abilities that will serve him well in any quantitative field. These aren't just "nice to have"—they're genuine mathematical advantages.

⚡ Variable Flexibility

I deliberately changed variables throughout our problems—swapping x for t, replacing n with k. Brenden rolled with every change, understanding the letter is just a placeholder. This symbolic fluency is rare.

🎯 No Fear of Fractions

Many students develop "fraction anxiety." Not Brenden. He attacked every fraction-heavy problem with confidence, never letting rational expressions slow him down.

🔍 Pattern Recognition

Working through series summations, Brenden demonstrated excellent pattern recognition—distinguishing arithmetic from geometric sequences and spotting underlying structures.

✨ Self-Correction

After comparing his work to mine, Brenden didn't wait for correction. He said, "Oh, I see what I did wrong!" This metacognitive awareness separates good students from great ones.

The Superpower in Disguise

Here's the pattern that needs adjustment—but first, a reframe: this isn't a weakness. It's a strength being misapplied.

Brenden is resourceful. When facing new problems, he looks back at similar ones for guidance. He reads context clues and searches for patterns. This is exactly what good problem-solvers do. The challenge? He's currently using specific formulas and data from previous problems rather than extracting the underlying concepts.

Example: In sigma notation problems, Brenden would take the an formula from problem #1 and apply it to problem #4—where an was different. He was pattern-matching on surface structure rather than deep structure.

The good news: Students who don't look back at examples often struggle to build connections. Brenden already has the connection-building instinct. He just needs to refine his filter—extracting the approach and concept rather than the specific numbers.

Growth Areas

Two smaller adjustments that will yield significant results:

Small Math Errors

Occasional computational mistakes when working quickly. Not conceptual errors—execution errors. Solution: systematic checking between steps and before final answers. In our relaxed session, these were minimal. Under test conditions, they might increase.

Concept Extraction

After completing problems, shift from "What was the answer?" to "What was the method?" This transforms someone who can follow examples into someone who can solve novel problems independently.

A Win Worth Celebrating

Brenden missed school for a wrestling event, which meant he missed the lesson on series summations—exactly what his class was working through when he returned. By the end of our session, he told me he felt confident in the material.

That moment demonstrated something crucial: Brenden can learn complex material quickly when given the right support. That's not a small thing. That's evidence of genuine mathematical ability.

Moving Forward: The Path to Stable B+

Based on this session, I'm confident about Brenden's trajectory. He has the ability, the right instincts, and the self-awareness to grow. What we're working on is consistency under pressure and refinement of already-strong problem-solving skills.

My recommendation for Brenden:

  • Ask more questions in class—even to paraphrase what the teacher just said
  • Request clarification on anything that feels even slightly unclear
  • Ask for one more example, even when you think you understand
  • Check if your approach would work before committing to it

Teachers secretly love this (even if we sigh at the sheer amount of interruptions). What looks like over-questioning is actually how students master material. It's active learning. In our sessions, we'll build on this foundation—emphasizing concept extraction, systematic checking, and tackling increasingly complex problems as confidence grows.

The goal isn't just a stable B. The goal is for Brenden to walk into class knowing he has the tools to handle whatever comes his way.
