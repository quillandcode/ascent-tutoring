2
Whiteboard Practice — Logic-Driven Side Lengths

Rung 2 moved from video problems to whiteboard work — my problems, my shapes, real-time solving. We started with basic composite configurations: rectangles and squares combined in straightforward ways.

The key skill here wasn't multiplication. It was logical deduction. When a composite shape only labels some of its sides, you have to calculate the unlabeled ones. For instance: if the total width is 10m and one segment is 4m, the remaining segment is 6m — because that's what has to be true for the shape to work.

total side − labeled segment = missing segment

PJ internalized this quickly. But something even better happened: he noticed that composite shapes can be divided in more than one way. The same irregular figure can be split into two rectangles with a horizontal cut, or two different rectangles with a vertical cut — and either approach gives you the same total area. Recognizing that there's no single "right" way to divide a shape is a sign of flexible mathematical thinking.

The Finish: By the end of Rung 2, PJ was working through whiteboard problems completely on his own — no guidance, no hints. He just solved them.

Status: Mastered. Multiple strategies understood.
3
Subtractive Area — The Curveball

Rung 3 was designed to surprise. So far, composite area had always meant adding the parts together. Rung 3 introduced a different kind of problem: what if you need to find the area of a shaded region — the part of a shape that remains after a hole has been cut out?

The setup: a large rectangle with a smaller rectangle removed from its interior. The shaded region is everything that's left. The shape looks composite, but the strategy isn't addition — it's subtraction.

🔧 The Initial Instinct

PJ's first move was to add the two areas together. That's the natural instinct — he'd been adding all session. His brain reached for the familiar tool. When I pointed to the shaded region and asked what specifically we were finding, something shifted. He looked at the picture again — really looked at it — and the logic clicked on its own. The shaded part isn't both rectangles. It's what's left after one is taken away.

The Insight: Subtract the inner shape from the outer shape. Once PJ made that connection, he didn't need another nudge. He solved every remaining subtractive problem independently — no hesitation, no errors.

Status: Mastered after a single redirect. Independent on all follow-up problems.
4
Circles & Semicircles — Reaching the Top

Rung 4 was the summit — and it was steep. Circles introduce a new formula, a new kind of measurement (radius), and an entirely new way of thinking about area. We tackled them in two stages.

Stage 1: A circle inside a square. The problem used the same subtractive logic from Rung 3 — find the area of the square, subtract the area of the circle. PJ handled this without trouble. The subtractive structure was familiar now; only the circle formula was new.

A = πr²

Stage 2: The semicircle problem. This one required discovery. A semicircle was sitting on top of a square, and we needed to find its area. I asked PJ: if a circle has area πr², what would half of a circle have? He worked through it — half the formula means half the result — and landed on the right answer through his own reasoning.

📏 The Radius Puzzle

The trickiest part: the problem didn't label the radius directly. The semicircle sat on top of the square, and the square's side length was given. PJ had to recognize that the diameter of the semicircle equaled the side of the square — and that the radius is half the diameter. Through guided questions, he deduced this himself. No formula was handed to him.

The Summit: PJ reasoned his way to the radius, applied the semicircle formula correctly, and solved the problem. Circles were introduced and mastered in a single session — a genuine accomplishment.

Status: Mastered. Circles and semicircles both handled successfully.

🗺️ Moving Forward

What to Practice

The conceptual work is done. The next step is fluency — solving problems fast, accurately, and without hesitation. Here's what will sharpen that:

Keep using the cross-out strategy. It works. Every time a composite shape gets divided, cross out the original side lengths. It costs one second and prevents real errors.

On any shaded region problem — stop and ask first: "What's the relationship between the shaded part and the total?" Additive or subtractive? Label it before calculating. That pause is what separates clean work from careless mistakes.

For circles: always find the radius first. Before touching πr², locate or calculate the radius. Write it down. Then apply the formula. This two-step habit prevents errors where the diameter accidentally gets used instead of the radius.

Long-term: PJ's ability to deduce missing information from context — like calculating the radius from the square's side — will serve him far beyond this topic. Geometry, trigonometry, and algebra all reward the same skill: asking "what else does this tell me?" Keep practicing that instinct.
